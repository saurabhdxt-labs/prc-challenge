# Research findings — what actually wins this challenge · 2026-09-08

Sources read in full, not skimmed: the 2026 challenge page, the 2025 winner announcement with the
judges' per-team repo reviews, the 2025 ranking rules, a review of the 2024 winning code, and the
NASA Ames taxi-out prediction study at Charlotte. Every number below is quoted from a source.

---

## 1. The competition is smaller and tighter than it looks

**2025 edition, the most recent complete one:**

|---|---|
| teams signed up | 179 |
| teams that actually submitted | **53** |
| total submissions | 2,127 |
| best final RMSE | 200.83 |

| rank | team | RMSE | gap to 1st |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | resourceful-quiver | 200.83 | — |
| 2 | jubilant-vase | 211.99 | +5.6% |
| 3 | outspoken-tornado | 219.85 | +9.5% |

| 10 | unique-quicksand | 256.27 | +27.6% |
| 12 | wise-watermelon | 350.98 | +74.8% |

**Three things follow.**

* **Seventy per cent of registrants never submit.** Finishing at all is most of the battle.
* **Ranks 3, 4 and 5 are separated by 0.6%.** The middle of the podium is a coin toss between
  competent entries, which is exactly the noise-floor problem our own tail analysis predicts.
* **First place is genuinely clear of the field** at 5.6% ahead of second. Winning is not a
  rounding error; it requires something the others did not do.

## 2. What the judges actually reward — and this is the biggest finding

Ranking is by RMSE, but **prize award is not**. Quoted from the 2025 rules:

> The Selection Committee will review and discuss the top ranking teams and select final Awardees
> considering **their final ranking score, the originality of the proposed model, [and] the open
> availability of the code and the relevant documentation provided.**

And the committee publishes a written review of every repository. Their language is unambiguous
about what they value. On the **winner**:

> "a well-founded and thoughtful methodology that is **strongly informed by domain knowledge
> rather than relying exclusively on machine-learning techniques** … Documentation is a major
> strength … The near-complete JOAS-style paper is clearly written, well structured"

On **third place**, criticised despite the score:

> "follows a **predominantly machine-learning–driven approach, with less emphasis on
> domain-specific feature engineering** … **limited evidence of domain-informed validation, such
> as checks for outliers or physically implausible values**"

On teams ranked 8 to 12, the recurring complaints are: hardcoded paths, Windows-only paths,
sparse or absent documentation, no explanation of design choices, and notebooks with no comments.

And note who got the *warmest* review in the entire field — **rank 11**:

> "bright-lobster stands out for its strong emphasis on **reproducibility, excellent
> documentation, and a well-defined entry point** … demonstrates deep domain awareness"

**The strategic conclusion.** The committee is a group of aviation performance analysts reading
code. They reward domain reasoning, honest data validation, reproducibility and a written paper.
The pre-registration discipline this project already runs — a censoring policy fixed before any
score, a planted-leak harness, mutation-rehearsed tests, provenance manifests, a frozen
prereg with an amendment log — is precisely the axis on which they separate teams, and it is the
axis on which most entrants are weakest. That is our edge, and it is not a modelling edge.

## 3. What won in 2024 and 2025, mechanically

**2024 (takeoff weight), from a review of the winning repositories:**

| rank | RMSE | approach |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Likeable Jelly (ENAC professors) | 1,561 | **LightGBM**, single model |
| 2 Faithful Engine (**hobbyists**) | 1,635 | **AutoGluon** AutoML |
| 3 Tiny Rainbow (ITA Brazil) | 2,218 | ensemble of CatBoost + LightGBM + XGBoost + NN |

Note that a **single LightGBM beat a four-model ensemble by 30%**. The difference was not the
estimator. The winner's edges were:

1. **A physics library** (OpenAP) turning trajectories into fuel-burn and mass estimates.
2. **External weather** (METAR) at the *destination*, chosen by domain reasoning about why it
   would matter.
3. **Bad timestamps identified and repaired** rather than trusted.
4. **Target reparameterisation**: they modelled `(TOW − EOW) / (MTOW − EOW)`, a dimensionless
   "load fraction", not raw weight, then converted back.

**2025 (fuel burn):** the winner again used domain knowledge and explicitly reused the prior
year's mass-estimator idea. Second place did the same plus an ensemble of three boosters.

**The pattern across both years: domain reformulation of the target and physics-derived features
beat estimator sophistication, every time.**

## 4. The taxi-out domain model, and what it gives us

The canonical decomposition (Simaiakis and Balakrishnan, *Transportation Science*; the queuing
literature the NASA study builds on) is:

> **taxi-out time = unimpeded taxi time + departure queue delay + ramp and taxiway congestion delay**

This is the direct analogue of the mass estimator that won the last two editions. It gives us a
principled target reparameterisation and a feature family at once.

**Unimpeded taxi time has a concrete, published operational definition.** From the NASA Ames
study at Charlotte:

> "the unimpeded taxi time was defined to be the **10th percentile of the taxi time distributions
> … for aircraft with the same gate, spot and runway combination**"

We can compute exactly this: `STAND_mvt` and `RUNWAY_mvt` are **0.0% null** in our data.

**The feature list that study selected**, after analysing a full year of surface surveillance:
terminal concourse, gate, spot, runway, departure fix, aircraft model, weight class, taxi
distance gate-to-runway, scheduled pushback time of day, **number of departures on the surface by
runway**, **number of arrivals on the surface by concourse**, and **unimpeded taxi time**.

Their reference figures at Charlotte: mean taxi-out 18.82 minutes, standard deviation 8.66
minutes, and **7.25% of flights discarded** for missing or non-positive taxi times. Our data is
far cleaner: 0.009% discarded.

They also found **no seasonal effect at Charlotte**. That will not transfer. Our evaluation is
January and July in Europe, where de-icing makes winter a different operating regime, and the
challenge chose those two months deliberately.

---

## 5. What this implies for us, stated as claims to be tested, not conclusions

1. **Reparameterise the target.** Predict excess over unimpeded taxi time, or the ratio to it,
   rather than raw seconds. This is the 2024 winner's move transposed. It should absorb the
   stand-and-runway geometry into the denominator and leave the model to learn congestion.
2. **The queue is the dominant feature family**, and both the queuing literature and the NASA
   feature list agree: count of departures already pushed back and not yet airborne at time T,
   and arrivals taxiing in. **This is also the feature the as-of-T rule is most dangerous for**,
   which the pre-registration already names.
3. **Weather is the single highest-value external dataset**, more so than in 2024, because
   de-icing in January is a direct additive taxi-out delay and visibility drives runway
   configuration. METAR for ten airports is small and openly licensed.
4. **Do not lead with an ensemble.** Two years running, a well-fed single gradient booster beat
   ensembles. Ensembling is a late, cheap increment, not a strategy.
5. **The paper and the repository are part of the prize decision**, not an afterthought. Ours
   should be written as we go, not at the end.
